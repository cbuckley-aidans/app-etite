Task A: School Water Bottle

Step 1 of 7

Start with the design intent

Before writing a design criterion, you need to understand the real problem. The design intent tells you who is affected and what they actually need.

Design Intent — Task A

“Year 8 students are complaining that their current bottles are too bulky for their bags and strain their shoulders during the 20-minute walk to school.”

This tells us two things: the bottle needs to fit in a bag and be light enough to carry comfortably for 20 minutes. These are the properties we need to measure.

Step 2 of 7

Read the flawed criterion

Now look at what was written. Can you spot why it would be impossible to use as a design target?

Current Criterion

“The water bottle must be easy to carry and not too heavy.”

Too Vague

“Easy to carry” and “not too heavy” are opinions, not measurements. Two different people would give two different answers about whether a bottle passes. A good criterion must give you a yes or no, no matter who is testing it.

Step 3 of 7

Apply the 4-question test

Run the flawed criterion through each question to see exactly where it breaks down.

Specific?

Does it name a precise property with a number or condition?

✗ No. “Not too heavy” is not a measurement.

Testable?

Could you run a test and get a strict yes or no?

✗ No. “Easy to carry” depends on who you ask.

Achievable?

Is it realistic for a student project to deliver?

✓ Probably yes, once the target is defined.

Relevant?

Does it connect directly to the design intent?

✓ Yes — weight and portability are the core problem.

Two questions pass, two fail. We need to rewrite the criterion so it passes all four.

Step 4 of 7

Fix “not too heavy”

The phrase “not too heavy” is an opinion. To make it testable, we need to replace it with a number that comes from the design intent and reflects a real user need.

Specific

Who are we designing for, and what load are they already carrying?

Year 8 students carry up to 5kg of books daily and walk 20 minutes. Adding another heavy item strains their shoulders.

Fix

Replace the vague phrase with a measurable target

The bottle must weigh no more than 300g when empty.

Step 5 of 7

Fix “easy to carry”

“Easy to carry” relates to the bag fitting problem in the design intent. We can make this testable by specifying a physical dimension that can be measured.

Testable

What does “easy to carry in a bag” actually mean physically?

The bottle must fit in the side pocket of a standard school bag without forcing the zip open or making the bag lopsided.

Fix

Turn that into a measurable dimension

The bottle must have a diameter of no more than 8cm so it fits in a standard school bag side pocket.

Step 6 of 7

Assemble the pieces

We now have two specific, testable targets. A good criterion can combine multiple measurements as long as each one is clear and verifiable on its own.

Specific

Weight target

No more than 300g when empty

Testable

Size target

Diameter no more than 8cm

Achievable

Is this realistic for a student project?

Yes — standard drink bottles already meet these dimensions, so the target is realistic.

Relevant

Does it directly address the design intent?

Yes — both measurements directly solve the “too bulky and too heavy” complaints.

Step 7 of 7

The improved criterion

Compare the two. The improved criterion gives a designer a clear, testable target. Anyone with a scale and a ruler could check whether a prototype passes.

Original (Flawed)

“The water bottle must be easy to carry and not too heavy.”

✓  Improved Criterion

“The bottle must weigh no more than 300g when empty and have a diameter of no more than 8cm so that it fits in the side pocket of a standard school bag.”

Notice how the improved criterion contains numbers. Anyone could pick up a bottle, put it on a scale and measure its diameter, and get a definitive pass or fail — no interpretation required.
