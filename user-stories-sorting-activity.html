Task A: Step by Step

Step 1 of 7

Start with the design intent

Before writing a user story, you need to understand the real problem you’re solving. The design intent tells you who is struggling and what the actual pain point is.

Design Intent

“Students often miss deadlines because the “Submit” button is buried in sub-menus and hard to find when rushing.”

This tells us: the person is a student under time pressure, and the problem is that a critical button is hard to locate quickly. Hold onto this — it’s our compass for building the story.

Step 2 of 7

Read the flawed story

Now look at the user story that was written for this intent. Something is off — can you spot it?

Current User Story

As a user, I want a search bar so that I can search for things.

Generic WHO Feature-Led WHAT Circular WHY

Three problems at once. This story could apply to almost anyone building almost anything. It gives a designer nothing useful to work from.

Step 3 of 7

Fix the WHO

The WHO tells us exactly who will use this feature. The more specific it is, the better we can design for their real situation.

WHO — the problem

“a user” — This could be anyone. It tells us nothing about their context, pressure, or constraints.

Ask: Who exactly from the design intent is struggling? What is their specific situation?

WHO

Who is the specific person in the design intent?

A student — and not just any student, but one who is rushing to submit before a deadline.

Step 4 of 7

Fix the WHAT

The WHAT should describe a goal or need, not a finished design solution. Saying “a search bar” locks the designer into one solution before they’ve even started.

WHAT — the problem

“a search bar” — This is a feature, not a goal. What does the student actually need to do?

Ask: What is the functional goal? What are they trying to accomplish, not which UI element do they want?

WHAT

What does the student actually need to do?

Find the Submit button quickly — without digging through sub-menus while under pressure.

Step 5 of 7

Fix the WHY

The WHY must explain a real human benefit — something that actually changes the person’s situation for the better. A circular WHY just repeats the WHAT in different words.

WHY — the problem

“so that I can search for things” — This is circular. It restates the want without explaining any benefit. Why does it matter that they can search?

Ask: What is the real cost if this problem isn’t solved? What changes for the better if it is?

WHY

What happens if the Submit button stays hidden?

The student misses the deadline and panics. The benefit is being able to submit on time, without stress.

Step 6 of 7

Assemble the pieces

Now we have all three ingredients. Let’s put them together using the formula.

WHO

Specific person in a real situation

a student who is rushing to submit work before a deadline

WHAT

Goal-oriented need, not a feature

find the Submit button from any screen without navigating sub-menus

WHY

Meaningful benefit that changes their situation

hand in on time without panicking

Step 7 of 7

The improved story

Compare the two. The improved story gives a designer a clear picture of who they’re designing for, what that person needs to accomplish, and why it matters.

Original (Flawed)

As a user, I want a search bar so that I can search for things.

✓  Improved Story

As a student who is rushing to submit work before a deadline, I want to find the Submit button from any screen without navigating sub-menus so that I can hand in on time without panicking.

Notice how the improved story opens multiple design possibilities — a persistent toolbar, a quick-access button, a shortcut — rather than locking in one solution before the thinking has even started.
